Happy Birthday Augustine (354-430 CE)

The famous procrastinator and overthinker who stole pears, failed spectacularly, at least in his desire to be good all the time, and finally decided to become a saint—then wrote a million words confessing it, all while trying to figure out how to be good. I actually read about him and resonated very well with him; I think a lot of overthinking, introspective people would too.

Foundational Setup (Making him human, despite his philosophical genius)

So, imagine you’re a smart, ambitious, and well-educated young person in the Roman Empire:

  • Giving speeches for a living (a rhetoric professor—basically a debate and persuasion skills teacher).
  • Dating a woman you love (for 15 years!).
  • Fortunate in your career.
  • Mom is very religious and is in continuous prayer mode—hoping you would follow her choice of religion, Christianity.

You’re skeptical and wish to explore—saying something like, "Mom, it’s not a big deal, I’m into philosophy and exploration. I will get this sorted, just not now…"

The Problem

You are feeling literally empty. Something’s missing—even when you think you have everything that the world considers a good, successful life. You know what you want to do or should do to be a good person, but you keep messing up—you can’t stop doing things you later regret.

The comedic tension in his life was that he was too self-aware, but still did regretful stuff.

The Pear Incident

When he was a teenager, he and his friends stole some pears from a neighbor’s tree. They didn’t even eat the pears. They just threw them at pigs, as it wasn’t good.

Normal person: "Ah… I was a stupid kid, but it was fun."
Meanwhile, Augustine: writes entire chapters… "Holy Christ! WHY did I do this? I wasn’t hungry! I knew stealing was wrong, even the fruit wasn’t good…"

Thirty years later, he's a bishop. Respected. Holy. And he STILL can't stop thinking about those pears.

Straight-Faced/Feynman Translation

Augustine stole some pear fruits as a teen, but couldn’t forgive himself. This tells about his mind—he couldn’t easily let go of the past.

Imagine if Augustine was in Therapy

Therapist: “Gentleman, what brings you here?”
Augustine: “I stole pears when I was 16.”
Therapist: “Okay, you clearly don’t steal pears now. Have you done anything regretful recently?”
Augustine: “I have a lot of things. I literally am writing all of it. I guess this all started when the beginners of life (Adam, Eve) ate an apple from that tree.”
Therapist notes: “There is a pattern of some issue with fruits.”

Life Journey

  • Lives with his girlfriend.
  • They have a son together.
  • He’s into Manichaeism (a religion which says the world is a dualistic battle between opposites—good and evil, light and dark).
  • Successful career, but he feels lost.

The Transformational Crisis

He is sitting in a garden, feeling completely broken inside. He hears a child’s voice saying ‘tolle lege’—which meant “Take up and read”. Randomly, he opens a page and reads a passage about giving up worldly pleasures.

Dramatic conversion: He had a psychological breakdown of the structure in his mind and then became a Christian.

His Big Ideas

He was a very normal person. What made him a genius was the same thing that made him worried about everything—he pondered vague “WHY” questions, and sometimes came up with good answers. Honestly, who knows who would be considered a genius in the future? There’s a good chance that’s any of us—but we only know if we write our thoughts down. This idea inspired me into writing.

1. Original Sin

The problem: "WHY do I keep doing bad stuff even when I know it’s bad?"

Augustine's attempt: We are all born with some bugs in our operating system (mind) and blamed them on Adam and Eve—a classic overthinker move.

Feynman translation: Humans are capable of mistakes on purpose—sometimes just to fit in, appear cool, or feel accepted.

Modern examples: cheating on tests, exaggerating stories, or doing things you knew were wrong for fun or social approval.

2. Free Will vs God’s Plan

The problem: If God knows everything that’s going to happen, do we really have free will? If we truly have free will, how can God know the future? If God made everything, why does evil exist?

Augustine's attempt: Maybe we have free will, which is why we make mistakes or sin—but we need God’s grace to actually choose good. God gives it to whomever He chooses, making God unconditional.

3. Time

The problem: "What is time?"

Insight: The past doesn't exist anymore. The future doesn't exist yet. The present is just an instant with no fixed measure. Time exists in the mind: memory (past), attention (present), expectation (future).

The Transformation

Before conversion: "I want to be good… but not YET. God, give me chastity and continence, but not now!"

After conversion: "Okay God, I'm ALL IN. Take my entire life. I will become a priest. I will write millions of words about you. I'll never stop writing about sin."

Where He Went Too Deep

Everything is about sin:

  • Babies? SINFUL (born with original sin).
  • Sex? SINFUL (even in marriage, when it’s supposed to be normal).
  • That time you thought about sex? SINFUL.
  • Stole pears as a teen? FULL GUILT.

Feynman insight: Humans have an internal conflict between desires and ideals, and Augustine turned this into an entire narrative exploration.

End Note

Even if I don’t agree with everything, Augustine was one of the first to explore the “why” behind human weakness. That tension between what we know we should do and what we actually do is very real, and he dived deep into it. We don’t need to stress about every thought or desire like he did, but noticing these conflicts—and maybe writing about them—can be useful, like a mini autobiography of your brain, a mini therapy.
